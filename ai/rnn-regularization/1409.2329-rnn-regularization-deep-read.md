# Recurrent Neural Network Regularization

> **Why it matters:** It shows that dropout, which the authors say "does not work well with RNNs and LSTMs" when applied naively, becomes an effective regularizer for multi-layer LSTMs if it is applied only to the non-recurrent (layer-to-layer) connections, which lets much larger LSTMs be trained without overfitting.

Version read: arXiv v5 (dated 2015-02-19 on the abs page; first submitted 2014-09-08), PDF text.

## Problem

The introduction notes that RNNs give strong results in language modeling, speech recognition and machine translation, yet practitioners tend to keep RNNs small because large RNNs overfit, and existing regularizers give only small gains for RNNs. Dropout is the strongest regularizer for feedforward networks, but applied in the standard way to an RNN it perturbs the recurrent connections. The paper's argument is that this makes it hard for the LSTM to keep information over long time spans. Bayer et al. (2013), the paper says, argued that conventional dropout hurts RNNs because recurrence amplifies the noise. The question the paper answers is where to put dropout in an LSTM so that it regularizes without destroying the memory.

## Core design

**Notation.** `h_t^l` is the hidden state of layer `l` at timestep `t`. The LSTM follows the formulation of Graves et al. (2013), with a memory cell `c_t^l` and input, forget and output gates. The paper notes that its cell (Figure 1) differs slightly from Graves (2013).

**Dropout placement.** The dropout operator **D** sets a random subset of its argument to zero. In each layer's gate computation, **D** is applied to the input from the layer below, `h_t^{l-1}`, and *not* to the recurrent input from the same layer at the previous step, `h_{t-1}^l`. In Figure 2, dashed arrows mark the connections that get dropout (vertical, between layers) and solid arrows mark the ones that don't (horizontal, through time).

**Why it works (the L+1 argument).** Figure 3 traces information from an event at step t-2 to a prediction at step t+2. On the vertical path it passes through dropout "exactly L+1 times", where L is the number of layers. That count does not depend on how many timesteps the information crosses. With standard dropout, the number of corruptions would grow with the time distance. Leaving the recurrent path clean keeps the LSTM's ability to memorize while still getting dropout's regularization on the deep (feedforward-in-depth) part of the network.

**What does not change.** It uses no new architecture, no extra parameters and no deterministic approximation. The authors expect the method to carry over to other RNN architectures as well.

**Training recipe (PTB).**
- Both regularized models have two layers and are unrolled for 35 steps, with minibatch 20 and hidden states initialized to zero.
- Medium: 650 units per layer, uniform init in [-0.05, 0.05], 50% dropout on non-recurrent connections, 39 epochs, learning rate 1. After 6 epochs the rate is divided by 1.2 each epoch, and gradient norm (normalized by minibatch size) is clipped at 5.
- Large: 1500 units per layer, uniform init in [-0.04, 0.04], 65% dropout, 55 epochs, learning rate 1. After 14 epochs the rate is divided by 1.15 each epoch, and gradient norm is clipped at 10.
- Non-regularized baseline: two layers of 200 units, init in [-0.1, 0.1], learning rate 1 for 4 epochs, then halved each epoch for a total of 13 epochs. It uses minibatch 20 and is unrolled for 20 steps.

**Other tasks.**
- MT (WMT'14 En→Fr, "selected" subset): 4 hidden layers, with 1000-unit layers and embeddings, optimal dropout 0.2 and beam size 12.
- Speech: frame-level acoustic modeling on an internal Google Icelandic dataset. The paper gives no model size or training details.
- Captioning: applies the dropout variant to the Vinyals et al. (2014) caption model on MSCOCO. The pretrained CNN is not trained on the caption data, so dropout is tested only on the LSTM.

## Limitations

- The paper is an empirical recipe. It offers no theory beyond the L+1 corruption-count argument, and it reports no ablation that applies dropout to recurrent connections for comparison.
- On translation, the regularized LSTM still does not beat the phrase-based LIUM system (Table 3).
- On captioning, the paper says "using an ensemble eliminates the gains attained by dropout". An ensemble of 10 non-regularized models matches or beats the single regularized model (Table 4), and no regularized ensemble is reported.
- The speech result is frame accuracy on an internal dataset, with training/validation columns only. The paper gives no model or training details, so it cannot be reproduced from the paper.
- (Own analysis) The PTB baseline differs from the regularized models in more than dropout. It is much smaller (200 vs 650/1500 units), uses a different learning-rate schedule, and is unrolled for 20 instead of 35 steps. The comparison is therefore "best tuned non-regularized" vs "larger regularized", not a controlled one-variable ablation.
- (Own analysis) Dropout rates are tuned per task and per size (50%, 65%, 0.2), so the method adds a hyperparameter to search over.

## Relation to other systems

- **Bayer et al. (2013):** the paper calls this the only prior work on dropout for RNNs. Bayer et al. focus on marginalized dropout (Wang & Manning, 2013), a noiseless deterministic approximation, and argue that conventional dropout fails in RNNs because recurrence amplifies noise. This paper keeps ordinary stochastic dropout and avoids the amplification by restricting it to a subset of connections.
- **Pham et al. (2013):** the paper says they developed the same method independently and applied it to handwriting recognition. This paper's contribution is to show it across language modeling, speech, MT and captioning.
- **Pascanu et al. (2013), Cheng et al., Mikolov (2012), Mikolov & Zweig (2012):** these are the PTB baselines in Table 1. The large regularized single model (78.4) is below the cited single-model numbers and the Mikolov (2012) averaging result. The 38-model regularized ensemble (68.7) is below the Mikolov & Zweig (2012) combination with dynamic RNNs and n-gram models (72.9).
- **LIUM SMT system (Schwenk et al., 2011):** this phrase-based system remains ahead of the regularized LSTM on En→Fr BLEU (Table 3).
- **Vinyals et al. (2014) caption model / Sutskever et al. (2014) sequence-to-sequence:** these are the host models the dropout variant is applied to for captioning and translation. The method plugs into them without architectural change.

## Takeaways for practice

(Own analysis throughout.)
- Regularization placement decides which model size you can usefully train. In this paper the best non-regularized PTB LSTM is 200 units/layer, while dropout makes 1500 units/layer the best single model. When sizing GPU jobs, the model you can use is limited by overfitting as well as by memory and compute.
- The compute cost of the bigger model is reported plainly: 2-3 hours, half a day and a full day on one K20 for the three PTB models. Capacity planning should budget for the larger configuration that regularization unlocks, not the baseline.
- The method keeps the recurrent path deterministic and masks only the per-timestep layer inputs. These are dense, non-sequential tensors, so the recurrence itself needs no change. That makes the change easy to retrofit into an existing training stack.
- Ensembles are a strong competitor. The 10-model non-regularized ensemble beats the single regularized model on captioning, and the 38-model regularized ensemble is the best PTB result. For inference platforms, weigh one large regularized model against N smaller models served together. The paper reports quality only, not serving cost.
- Recipe details matter and should be versioned with the job: per-size dropout rate, uniform init range, delayed learning-rate decay, and gradient-norm clipping normalized by minibatch size.
