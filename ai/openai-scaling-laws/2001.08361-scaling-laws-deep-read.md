# Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

> **Why it matters:** It turned "how big a model, how much data, what batch size, for this GPU budget" into fitted power laws, and it fed directly into how large training runs were sized and budgeted.

## Problem

Training a large language model costs a fixed amount of compute. The team running it has to split that compute between model size N, the number of training tokens D, the batch size B and the number of optimizer steps S. The authors ask how the cross-entropy loss of a decoder-only Transformer depends on N, D and training compute C, alone and jointly, and what compute-optimal allocation follows.

## Core design

The paper is an empirical study, not a system. Its "design" is the experimental method and the fitted functional forms.

**Setup (§2.2, §2.3, §3).**
- Data: WebText2, about 2.29×10^10 tokens, with 6.6×10^8 tokens held out for test. Byte-pair encoding with n_vocab = 50257. Loss is averaged over an n_ctx = 1024 context.
- Optimizer: Adam for most runs and Adafactor above 1B parameters, because of memory. 3000-step linear warmup, then cosine decay to zero.
- Default run: 2.5×10^5 steps at 512 sequences × 1024 tokens per batch.
- Sweep: models from 768 to 1.5B non-embedding parameters, datasets from 22M to 23B tokens.

**Accounting (§1.3, §2.1, Table 1).**
- N counts non-embedding parameters only. For the standard shape, N = 12·n_layer·d_model² (Eq. 2.1).
- Training compute is estimated as C ≈ 6N FLOPs per token, so C ≈ 6NBS. This drops the context-dependent attention term.
- Compute is reported in PF-days, where 1 PF-day = 8.64×10^19 FLOPs.
- Excluding embedding parameters is what makes the trends collapse onto one curve.

**Single-variable laws (§1.2, §3).** Loss is a power law in each variable when the other two are not the bottleneck:
- L(N) = (N_c/N)^α_N (Eq. 1.1)
- L(D) = (D_c/D)^α_D (Eq. 1.2)
- L(C_min) = (C_c^min/C_min)^α_C^min (Eq. 1.3)

Model shape (depth, width, heads, feed-forward ratio) matters weakly within broad ranges (§3.1).

**Joint law and overfitting (§4).**
- L(N,D) = [(N_c/N)^(α_N/α_D) + D_c/D]^α_D (Eq. 1.5 / 4.1).
- The overfitting penalty depends on N^0.74/D, which gives a data requirement of D ≳ (5×10^3)·N^0.74 (Eq. 4.4).

**Batch size and training time (§5).**
- The paper uses the step/example trade-off (S/S_min − 1)(E/E_min − 1) = 1 (Eq. 5.1) and defines the critical batch B_crit = E_min/S_min (Eq. 5.2).
- It finds that B_crit depends only on the loss, not on model size (§5.1): B_crit(L) ≈ B_*/L^(1/α_B) (Eq. 5.3).
- Runs at any batch size are mapped onto S_min (minimum steps) and C_min (minimum compute).
- Learning curves then fit L(N,S_min) = (N_c/N)^α_N + (S_c/S_min)^α_S (Eq. 5.6).
- This form gives a lower bound on the early-stopping step (Eq. 5.7).

**Compute-optimal allocation (§6, App. B).**
- Combining the laws gives N ∝ C^(α_C^min/α_N), B ∝ C^(α_C^min/α_B), S ∝ C^(α_C^min/α_S) and D = B·S (Eq. 1.7), with α_C^min = 1/(1/α_S + 1/α_B + 1/α_N) (Eq. 1.8, 6.4, B.7).
- The prescription: put most extra compute into a bigger model, train at about the critical batch size, and stop well short of convergence. Appendix B.2 puts the stopping point at about α_N/α_S ≈ 10% above the converged loss (Eq. B.5).

## Limitations

- **No theory (App. C).** The laws are fits. The paper says they lack a theoretical basis, especially the model-size and compute exponents.
- **Constants are tied to the setup (Table 5).** The constants depend on the tokenization and the dataset, which is WebText2 with BPE at n_ctx = 1024. They are not universal values.
- **Narrow loss range for B_crit (App. C).** The authors do not trust the B_crit(L) prediction far outside the loss range they explored.
- **Untested regimes (App. C).** The small-data regime, regularization and data augmentation were not studied thoroughly. Other hyperparameters, such as initialization scale and momentum, might matter. The learning rate was tuned, but the optimal rate depends on the target loss.
- **Compute estimate breaks at long context (App. C).** C ≈ 6NBS omits the n_ctx-dependent term, which may confound compute scaling at very large context lengths.
- **Self-contradiction at large scale (§6.3).** The data needed to avoid overfitting grows as D ∝ N^0.74 ∝ C_min^0.54 (Eq. 6.6), much faster than the compute-optimal D ∝ C^0.27. The trends must therefore break down near C* ~ 10^4 PF-days, which the paper calls "highly uncertain, varying by an order of magnitude".
- **The paper flags its own data trend as unstable (§6.3).** The overfitting-based requirement D ∝ N^0.74 ∝ C^0.54 grows faster than the compute-optimal D ∝ C^0.27, so the two cannot both hold indefinitely; the paper expects its trends to break down near C* ~ 10^4 PF-days. (Own analysis) Treat the N ∝ C^0.73 split as a fit to this paper's setup, not a law to copy.

## Relation to other systems

- **Hestness et al. 2017 (§7).** The paper calls this the closest prior work. Hestness et al. found super-linear growth of the data requirement with model size. This paper finds sub-linear growth (N^0.74).
- **EfficientNet (§7).** EfficientNet scales depth and width with a power-law relation between them. For language models, this paper finds that the power should be roughly one and that shape matters much less than total scale.
- **McCandlish et al. 2018, gradient noise scale (§1.1, §5.1).** The B_crit analysis builds directly on this work. The new result is that B_crit depends on the loss reached and not on model size.
- **GPipe and Mesh-TensorFlow (§8).** The paper notes that pipelining deep models eventually requires larger batches as devices are added, while wide models split more easily across workers. It calls for more work on model parallelism.
- **Recurrent and Universal Transformers (App. D.2, Fig. 17).** At equal parameter count these do slightly better, but they do slightly worse per FLOP. Parameter reuse does not escape the compute law.

## Takeaways for practice

- **Budget in FLOPs per token.** C ≈ 6N per training token (§2.1) is a good first-order estimate for dense Transformers. It turns a GPU-hour budget into a token budget once you know the cluster's sustained FLOP/s. Add the attention term back for long-context runs (App. C).
- **Pick the batch from the loss you are targeting, not from the model size (§5.1).** B_crit ∝ C^0.24 (Table 6), and §1.1 reports roughly 1–2M tokens for the largest models. Going far above B_crit buys little speed-up per extra GPU. Going below it wastes wall-clock time.
- **Serial steps barely grow with compute (S ∝ C^0.03, Table 6).** Most of a larger budget must come from parallelism: more data parallelism through larger batches, plus model parallelism for larger N. (Own analysis) Capacity planning should expect the job size, not the run length, to scale with the budget.
- **Choose shape for hardware efficiency (§3.1).** A 40× range of aspect ratio changes loss only slightly. (Own analysis) Choose d_model, the head count and n_layer to suit the hardware: matmul tile efficiency, tensor-parallel degree, pipeline stages. Wider models are easier to shard (§8).
- **Model size has a wide tolerance band.** Sizes from 0.6× to 2.2× optimal cost about 20% more compute (Fig. 12). This leaves room to round N to a size that fits memory and parallel layout.
- **Use early curves to triage runs (§1.1, §5).** Learning curves follow predictable power laws, so a short prefix of a run can be extrapolated to forecast final loss. (Own analysis) Use this to kill or resize bad runs early and to fit your own constants on small pilot runs before committing the cluster.
- **Do not copy the data split.** (Own analysis) The paper's split (N ∝ C^0.73, D ∝ C^0.27) favours big models on few tokens, and §6.3 itself expects it to break down at large compute. Refit on your own data, tokenizer and schedule. Plan for inference cost too, since a smaller model trained on more tokens is cheaper to serve.
