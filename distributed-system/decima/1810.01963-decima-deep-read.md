# Learning Scheduling Algorithms for Data Processing Clusters

> **Why it matters:** Decima showed that a graph neural network trained with policy-gradient RL can learn DAG-aware, parallelism-aware cluster scheduling policies that beat carefully tuned heuristics on Spark. It became a standard reference point for learned schedulers.

Source read: arXiv v4 (2019-08-21), which carries the SIGCOMM '19 ACM reference block.

## Problem

Spark jobs are DAGs of stages, and each stage has many parallel tasks. Production schedulers use general heuristics (FIFO, fair sharing, shortest-job-first) that ignore DAG structure and how well each job scales with parallelism (§1). Dependency-aware ordering is hard to get right algorithmically, and jobs scale very differently. In Fig. 2, TPC-H Q9 on 100 GB keeps speeding up to about 40 tasks, while Q2 stops gaining at 20 (§2.2).

A scheduler has to decide three things (§3): how many executors each job gets, which stage runs next, and which task runs on a freed executor. Decima learns the first two from a high-level objective such as average JCT. Task placement stays with Spark.

## Core design

**Agent loop (§4, §5.2).** Decima runs as a scheduling agent. It is invoked whenever the set of runnable stages changes: a stage runs out of tasks, a stage completes and unlocks its children, or a job arrives. On each invocation it observes all jobs' DAGs plus cluster state and emits one action.

**Graph neural network state encoding (§5.1).**
- Each stage node v of job i has a feature vector x_v. The features are remaining tasks, average task duration, executors currently working on the node, available executors, and whether the available executors are local to the job (§6.1).
- Per-node embeddings come from message passing that flows from children to parents, starting at the DAG leaves: e_v = g[Σ_{u∈children(v)} f(e_u)] + x_v (Eq. 1). The functions f and g are small neural networks shared across all nodes and all message-passing steps, so one model handles DAGs of any shape and size.
- The second non-linearity g is critical: without it the network cannot compute critical-path quantities, which need a max over children.
- Two summary levels reuse Eq. 1 with their own f and g. A per-job summary node has all of the job's stages as children and gives y^i. A global summary node has the job summaries as children and gives z. That makes six non-linear transforms in total (§5.1).

**Two-dimensional action (§5.2).**
- *Stage selection:* a score function q(e_v, y^i, z) is computed for every runnable node, followed by a softmax over the runnable set (Eq. 2).
- *Parallelism limit:* for the chosen stage's job, a function w(y^i, z, l) scores each candidate executor limit l, followed by a softmax. One w is shared across jobs and limits because l is an input. The limit must exceed the job's current allocation, so every action assigns at least one new executor.
- Parallelism is controlled per job, not per stage, which shrinks the policy space. Fig. 15a reports this matching stage-level control while training faster.
- In the multi-resource setting (§7.3) the action gains a third component, the executor class (memory size).

**Training (§5.3, App. B–C).**
- The algorithm is REINFORCE with a baseline (Eq. 3). For average JCT the reward after action k is r_k = −(t_k − t_{k−1})·J_k, where J_k is the number of jobs in the system during that interval. By Little's law, minimizing the time-averaged job count minimizes average JCT. The continuous-arrival case uses an average-reward formulation (App. B).
- *Input-dependent baseline:* baselines are averaged only over episodes that share the same job arrival sequence. The paper says this removes the variance that the arrival process causes.
- *Curriculum plus memoryless termination:* episodes start short and grow during training. Episode length is drawn from an exponential distribution, so the agent cannot learn to exploit a predictable episode end, for example by starving large jobs.
- The simulator models slower first-wave tasks, the JVM start-up delay when executors move between jobs, and slowdowns at high parallelism (§6.2).

**Integration (§6.1).** Decima is a pluggable RPC scheduling service that Spark's DAG schedulers and master call at scheduling events and job arrivals.

## Limitations

From the paper (§8 unless noted):
- **Generalization.** Table 2 shows a policy trained on a mismatched (anti-skewed) workload doing worse than the tuned heuristic. Training on mixed workloads, or adding interarrival hints, recovers part of the gap. The authors suggest training on worst-case or adversarial workloads and adapting the policy online.
- **No preemption.** Decima never preempts running tasks.
- **Single objective.** Other objectives need a reshaped reward. Fairness is not an objective (§9, in the comparison with Carbyne).
- **Simulator dependence.** Training happens in a simulator whose shared-cluster tail error reaches 20% (App. D), and the multi-resource and Alibaba results come from simulation, not the testbed (§7.3).
- **Partial control.** Task-level placement stays with Spark (§3).

(Own analysis) The policy takes per-stage task durations and remaining-task counts as inputs, and estimating these for new or ad-hoc jobs is a separate problem. A learned policy is also harder to audit than a heuristic, and the paper offers no interpretability tooling.

## Relation to other systems

Named in the paper (§7, §9):
- **Graphene:** schedules DAGs with heuristics but cannot set appropriate parallelism levels. Decima learns both stage order and per-job parallelism. It is compared against Graphene*, an adaptation to discrete executor classes.
- **Tetris:** greedy multi-resource packing that maximizes the dot product of requested and available resources. It has no DAG or JCT awareness, and Decima beats it by 52% on the Alibaba trace.
- **Carbyne:** Decima learns similar altruistic-style policies but does not target fairness.
- **DeepRM:** an earlier RL scheduler that handles only single-task jobs. Decima's GNN extends this line to DAG jobs and continuous arrivals.
- **Tetrisched / Firmament:** these need user-supplied constraints (a solver-based approach) or an administrator-configured policy. Decima learns from a high-level objective instead.
- **Device placement (Mirhoseini et al.):** scans graph nodes with RNNs. Decima uses a GNN, which scales and handles variable DAG sets.

## Takeaways for practice

All bullets are (Own analysis), drawn from the results above.
- Per-job parallelism control is the most valuable lever in the ablation. Even without RL, estimating each job's scaling curve and capping executors accordingly is worth building.
- Encoding state as a graph (shared message-passing functions plus job-level and global summaries) lets one small model, about 50 KB here, handle variable numbers of jobs and DAG shapes. The pattern carries over to schedulers for multi-stage training or inference pipelines.
- For RL on stochastic arrivals, variance control decides whether training works at all: fixed arrival sequences per iteration, input-dependent baselines, and memoryless episode termination.
- A learned policy needs a simulator that captures the overheads that matter here: executor moves, first-wave slowness and parallelism slowdowns. Validate the simulator against real runs before trusting the gains.
- Train on mixed workloads or give the policy workload hints, and keep a tuned heuristic (here, weighted fair with a tuned exponent) as baseline and fallback. A policy trained on one load regime can lose to that heuristic when the regime shifts.
