# Homa: a receiver-driven low-latency transport protocol using network priorities

## TL;DR

Homa is a receiver-driven transport protocol that uses in-network priority queues and controlled overcommitment to achieve exceptionally low latency for short messages while maintaining high throughput for large messages in datacenter networks.

## Problem & Motivation

Datacenter networks need to support a mix of short latency-sensitive messages (e.g., RPCs) and large throughput-intensive flows. Existing transport protocols (e.g., TCP, DCTCP) struggle to provide low tail latency for short messages under high load, often suffering from head-of-line blocking and inefficient priority usage. The challenge is to deliver microsecond-scale tail latency for short messages while preserving high utilization and fairness for large flows.

## Key Ideas

- Receiver-driven flow control: receivers assign priorities and grant credits to senders, enabling precise latency control.
- Dynamic priority allocation: receivers map each message to a priority level based on its size and remaining bytes, using in-network priority queues to preempt large flows.
- Controlled overcommitment: receivers intentionally overcommit downlink bandwidth to keep links busy, relying on priority scheduling to resolve contention.
- Two-phase scheduling: short messages are sent immediately with high priority; large messages are rate-limited and use lower priorities.

## System Design

Homa is a transport protocol implemented at the end hosts. Each receiver maintains per-sender state and dynamically assigns a priority to each incoming message based on its size and progress. Senders transmit packets with the assigned priority. Switches use strict priority queues (e.g., 8 levels) to schedule packets: higher-priority packets (short messages) are served before lower-priority ones (large messages). Receivers grant credits to senders to control injection rate, and overcommit the downlink to ensure full utilization. Flow control is receiver-driven: receivers decide when to grant more credits based on available buffer space and priority scheduling.

## Evaluation

The paper evaluates Homa using a Linux kernel implementation on a 10 Gbps testbed and ns-3 simulations. Workloads include synthetic RPC workloads and realistic datacenter traffic patterns. Baselines: TCP, DCTCP, pFabric, pHost, PIAS, NDP. Headline results: 99th percentile RTT <15 μs for short messages at 80% load (100x lower than best published measurements). In simulations, Homa matches pFabric's latency and outperforms pHost, PIAS, and NDP for most message sizes. Homa sustains higher load than pFabric, pHost, and PIAS.

## Takeaways

- Receiver-driven priority allocation can achieve near-optimal tail latency for short messages without centralized coordination.
- Controlled overcommitment is key to maintaining high utilization while preserving low latency.
- In-network priority queues are a powerful primitive for datacenter transport, but require careful integration with end-host flow control.
- Homa demonstrates that a clean-slate transport can outperform many prior proposals in both latency and throughput.

## Related Work

Homa is positioned against prior datacenter transport protocols: pFabric (priority-based with in-network scheduling), pHost (receiver-driven but without priorities), PIAS (priority-based with static thresholds), and NDP (receiver-driven with in-network scheduling). Homa combines receiver-driven control with dynamic priority allocation and overcommitment, achieving better latency and load tolerance than these approaches.

## Open Questions

- How does Homa perform with very large numbers of flows or extreme incast? The paper does not deeply explore scalability limits.
- The protocol requires switch support for many priority levels; deployment may be limited by hardware capabilities.
- Interaction with congestion control and fairness for long flows under high load could be further studied.
- Homa's receiver-driven design may introduce overhead for receivers under high load; the paper does not quantify this.
