# Kimi k1.5: Scaling Reinforcement Learning with LLMs — RL infrastructure

> **Why it matters:** One of the first production reports of long-chain-of-thought RL at 128k context, and its §2.6 describes the concrete plumbing (partial rollouts, GPU-sharing between Megatron and vLLM in one Kubernetes pod, a crun-based code sandbox) that makes such runs feasible.

## Problem

Kimi k1.5 is a multimodal LLM trained with RL to produce long chains of thought. From a systems view, long-CoT RL makes rollouts very long and very uneven. A synchronous loop would leave GPUs waiting on the longest trajectory. The trainer (Megatron) and the generator (vLLM) want different parallelism layouts. Inference capacity has to grow and shrink across phases. Code rewards need many short-lived, isolated executions.

## Core design

### Algorithm, briefly (§2.3–2.4)
- The optimiser is a variant of online policy mirror descent with relative-entropy regularisation. The baseline is the mean reward of k samples, and there is **no value network** (§2.3.2). Rewards are binary: test cases for code, a CoT reward model for free-form math (§2.3.1, §2.3.5).
- The recipe adds a length penalty (§2.3.3), curriculum and prioritised sampling (§2.3.4), and four long2short methods that distil long-CoT behaviour into short-CoT models: merging, shortest rejection sampling, DPO and long2short RL (§2.4).

### Iterative synchronous RL loop (§2.6.1, Fig. 3a)
- The design is modelled on game-playing RL systems (AlphaGo, AlphaStar, OpenAI Five). Each iteration has a **rollout phase** and a **training phase**.
- A central **master** coordinates **rollout workers**. Their trajectories go into a **replay buffer**, which decorrelates samples. **Trainer workers** read from the buffer and update the weights.
- The master also routes data to the reward models and to a code-execution service for execution-based rewards.

### Partial rollouts (§2.6.2, Fig. 3b)
- Each rollout iteration has a **fixed output-token budget**. A trajectory that is still unfinished at the budget is saved to the replay buffer and continued in a later iteration. One long response is therefore split into segments that span iterations *n−m … n*.
- Only the segment generated in the current iteration needs on-policy computation. The earlier segments are reused from the buffer. The paper says rollout workers "operate asynchronously", so short trajectories are not blocked behind long ones.
- Some segments can be excluded from the loss.
- **Repeat detection**: the system finds repeated sequences in generated text and stops them early. It can also add extra penalties for them, which saves rollout compute spent on degenerate loops.

### Hybrid deployment of training and inference (§2.6.3, Fig. 4)
- The paper names three goals that existing work did not meet together: (1) support for complex, differing parallelism strategies (Megatron's layout can differ from vLLM's), (2) minimal idle GPU time, and (3) dynamic scaling of inference.
- **Colocation**: Megatron and vLLM run in separate containers inside **one Kubernetes pod**, as sidecar containers that share all of the pod's GPUs.
- **Phase switch**: after a training step, Megatron offloads its GPU memory and prepares to send its current weights. vLLM starts with dummy weights and loads the real ones, which are transferred from Megatron through **Mooncake**. When the rollout finishes, the checkpoint engine stops all vLLM processes. Once that memory is free, Megatron loads its state back and starts the next training round.
- **Checkpoint Engine**: a shim that manages the vLLM process lifecycle. Operations and status are broadcast through a global metadata system run on **etcd**. vLLM memory is hard to free completely because of CUDA graphs, NCCL buffers and NVIDIA drivers, so to keep changes to vLLM small the engine kills and restarts vLLM instead of shrinking it in place. The paper says this also helps fault tolerance.
- **Weight path**: each Megatron worker converts its checkpoint shard to Hugging Face format in shared memory. The conversion resolves pipeline and expert parallelism, so only tensor parallelism is left for vLLM to handle. The shards are registered in the metadata system and moved between peer nodes by Mooncake over RDMA.

### Code sandbox (§2.6.4)
- A secure execution service that switches container images to support MultiPL-E, DMOJ Judge Server, Lean and Jupyter Notebook. It runs on Kubernetes behind HTTP, relying on Kubernetes restarts and rolling updates for availability.
- Startup optimisations: **crun** instead of Docker, **pre-created cgroups**, and an **overlay filesystem with a tmpfs upper layer** to control disk writes.

## Limitations

- **No end-to-end systems evaluation.** §2.6 is a design description. It reports no rollout throughput, GPU utilisation, scaling curve or cluster size, and does not measure how much partial rollouts save. The only measured systems numbers are the phase-switch times and the sandbox micro-benchmarks.
- **Staleness is not analysed.** *(Own analysis.)* A trajectory built from segments generated across iterations *n−m … n* mixes tokens from several policy versions. The paper calls the loop "iterative synchronous", yet its rollout workers run asynchronously. It never says how large m gets, whether stale segments are importance-corrected, or which segments are excluded from the loss.
- **Colocation forces time-slicing.** *(Own analysis.)* Training and inference share one pod's GPUs and alternate, so the design cannot overlap generation with training. Killing and restarting vLLM every iteration also adds startup cost, which the paper bounds only by the <1 min figure.
- **Dependence on external components.** Weight transfer relies on Mooncake over RDMA and on etcd for coordination. The paper does not discuss what happens when either fails or slows down.
- **Thin sandbox security discussion.** The paper calls the sandbox "secure" but gives no threat model. The isolation described rests on standard containers (crun with cgroups), with no stronger boundary such as a microVM or gVisor-style layer. *(The point about stronger isolation is own analysis.)*
- **Hard to reproduce.** *(Own analysis.)* The paper describes the infrastructure but does not release it as code, and does not give cluster sizes or configurations, so the design cannot be re-run from the text.

## Relation to other systems

- **HybridFlow / verl (EuroSys 2025).** Its abstract describes a hybrid single-controller/multi-controller programming model and a 3D-HybridEngine that reshards the actor between training and generation with no memory redundancy. Kimi also colocates training and generation, but it solves the layout mismatch differently: it uses two separate engines (Megatron, vLLM) in sidecar containers, converts the checkpoint to HF format and restarts vLLM, instead of resharding in place inside one engine.
- **AReaL (NeurIPS 2025).** Its abstract describes fully decoupling generation from training: rollout workers stream continuously, trainers update as soon as a batch is ready, and a staleness-aware PPO variant handles old samples. Kimi's partial rollouts attack the same long-tail problem but keep an iteration boundary and a token budget. Kimi does not describe AReaL's explicit staleness control or its off-policy correction.
- **StreamRL (arXiv 2025).** Its abstract argues that colocation limits scalability. StreamRL disaggregates generation and training onto separate (possibly heterogeneous, cross-datacenter) resources and overlaps them through stream generation, with an output-length ranker for long-tail samples. Kimi takes the opposite deployment choice (one pod, time-sliced GPUs). Its answer to long tails is the token budget plus repeat detection, not length prediction.
- **OpenRLHF (arXiv 2024).** Its abstract describes a Ray + vLLM + DeepSpeed + HF Transformers framework built for ease of use and speed. Like Kimi, it pairs vLLM generation with a separate training engine, but it is an open general-purpose RLHF framework. Kimi's stack is internal, Megatron-based and specialised for long-CoT with execution rewards.

## Takeaways for practice

- **Cap rollout length per iteration, not per trajectory.** A fixed token budget per iteration plus resumable trajectories in the replay buffer turns long-tail stragglers into carry-over work. Track how many policy versions each trajectory spans and decide on correction or loss masking up front.
- **Detect repetition inside the generator.** Stopping degenerate loops early reclaims rollout GPU time.
- **Restarting can beat freeing memory.** If an inference engine will not release GPU memory cleanly (CUDA graphs, NCCL buffers), killing and relaunching it from a fast weight source is a pragmatic way to time-share GPUs.
- **Use a neutral checkpoint format for engine handoff.** Resolving pipeline and expert parallelism on the trainer side and handing off HF-format shards in shared memory, moved over RDMA, separates trainer and inference parallelism.
- **Scheduler model: one pod, two containers.** Kubernetes sidecars sharing all GPUs, coordinated through etcd, colocate phases simply, at the cost of no overlap.
- **Execution-reward sandboxes are a container-churn problem.** crun, pre-created cgroups and tmpfs-backed overlays are the levers (§2.6.4). Measure containers per second per node, not only latency.
