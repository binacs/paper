# A System for Massively Parallel Hyperparameter Tuning

> **Why it matters:** ASHA turns successive halving into a barrier-free, promote-whenever-possible scheduling rule, which makes aggressive early stopping work with hundreds of workers, stragglers and dropped jobs, and the paper pairs it with concrete production lessons from Determined AI.

## Problem

The paper targets the "large-scale regime": far more configurations to evaluate than workers, expensive models, shared clusters (Section 1). Bayesian optimization is inherently sequential, and parallel BO via constant-liar heuristics underperforms its sequential version (Section 2). Successive halving (SHA) and Hyperband fit the regime better, but parallel SHA is synchronous: each rung waits for all its jobs, so it is sensitive to stragglers and dropped jobs, and its top-1/η estimates do not improve across brackets (Section 3.1). The paper also covers interface, autoscaling, scheduling and reproducibility of a tuning service (Section 5).

## Core design

### Successive halving (Section 3.1, Algorithm 1)
- Inputs: number of configurations n, minimum resource r, maximum resource R, reduction factor η ≥ 2, minimum early-stopping rate s. s_max = ⌊log_η(R/r)⌋.
- A bracket samples n configurations, trains each for r·η^s, keeps the top 1/η, multiplies the per-configuration resource by η, and repeats until R. Rung i holds ⌊n·η^(−i)⌋ configurations at resource r·η^(i+s). Lower s means more aggressive early stopping.
- Algorithm 1 asserts n ≥ η^(s_max − s), so at least one configuration reaches R.
- Synchronous parallel SHA: in the Figure 1 example (n=9, r=1, R=9, η=3), bracket 0 needs 3 × time(R) to return a fully trained configuration, because each rung is a barrier.

### Asynchronous promotion rule (Section 3.2, Algorithm 2)
- Whenever a worker is free, `get_job()` scans rungs from the highest promotable rung down to rung 0. In rung k, the candidates are the top ⌊|rung k|/η⌋ configurations by loss *seen so far*; the first candidate not already promoted is returned for training at rung k+1.
- If no rung has a promotable configuration, a new configuration is sampled at random and started in rung 0.
- Completed jobs only record their loss; nothing waits for a rung to fill. Configurations trained to R are not promoted, capping the rungs.
- Latency: with 9 machines in the Figure 1 example, ASHA returns a fully trained configuration in 13/9 × time(R). In general the paper bounds this by 2 × time(R), and by time(R) when training is iterative and configurations can be checkpointed and resumed (Section 3.2).
- n is not needed up front; it becomes a stopping criterion in production (Section 5.1).

### Mispromotions and horizons (Section 3.3)
- A mispromotion is a configuration promoted early that is not in the true top 1/η in hindsight. By analogy to empirical-CDF convergence (Dvoretzky et al., 1956), the paper argues the first rung has roughly √n mispromotions among n configurations; for later rungs the authors only *expect* dependence to be weak. An argument, not a theorem.
- Removing the rung cap extends ASHA to the infinite horizon; SHA would need the doubling trick. ASHA also reports the current best from intermediate losses instead of waiting for a bracket to finish.

### Stragglers and dropped jobs (Appendix A.1, Figure 7)
- Simulation with 25 workers: runtimes scaled by (1+|z|), z normal; jobs drop with probability p per time unit; η=4, r=1, R=256, n=256, s=0 for both ASHA and synchronous SHA.
- Under high variance and drop risk, ASHA trains more configurations to completion and returns a fully trained one faster: a straggler holds only its own slot.

### Productionizing in Determined AI (Section 5)
- **Interface (5.1):** random-search-like inputs; η=4; bracket s=0 has up to 5 rungs (r = R/256); default brackets s=0,1,2. n is the stopping criterion, split so each bracket gets equal total budget. Each bracket's bottom rung is capped at n_s, so at most k (workers) jobs are pending there; for n_s ≫ k, promotions are not blocked until the bracket is nearly complete.
- **Autoscaling (5.2):** multi-GPU scaling is sublinear, so per-configuration parallelism is capped using Paleo efficiency predictions: a per-model latency versus throughput trade-off.
- **Resource allocation (5.3):** instead of FIFO with static requests, a job's maximum parallelism starts at nκ (κ = per-configuration parallelism) and adapts to κ|S| for a stack S of pending tasks; a central fair-share scheduler with optional priority weights water-fills, redistributing allocation above a job's maximum.
- **Reproducibility (5.4):** checkpoints cover weights, optimizer, RNG and data-generator state; deterministic GPU math depends on the framework. Every promotion is logged, so rung membership and the task stack can be replayed for crash recovery or to add configurations to a bracket.

## Limitations

- The mispromotion analysis is heuristic (first rung only; later-rung dependence "expected to be weak"), and BOHB ends with a slightly better final configuration on benchmark 1 (§3.3, §4.2).
- New configurations are sampled at random (Algorithm 2); ASHA itself has no model-based search.
- PBT beats ASHA early on the modern LSTM benchmark before ASHA catches up (Appendix A.6), and on benchmark 2 PBT is comparable to ASHA (§4.2).
- The 10× speedup at 25 workers on benchmark 1 is attributed to task simplicity (§4.2).
- Vizier early-stopping results were omitted after its team reported a bug, so the Vizier comparison is without early stopping (§4.4, footnote).
- Reproducibility depends on framework support for deterministic GPU computation (§5.4).
- (Own analysis) Like any successive-halving method, ASHA assumes low-budget rankings predict high-budget ones; where early loss misleads, aggressive brackets hurt. The bottom-rung cap of §5.1 also reintroduces some blocking near each bracket's end, which the paper does not quantify.

Inconsistencies noticed in the paper:
- Appendix A.2 says Fabolas is compared on "4 different benchmarks" but lists three tasks (SVM, CIFAR-10 CNN, SVHN CNN).
- Appendix A.1 says ASHA beats synchronous SHA in Section 4.4, but Section 4.4 compares ASHA, asynchronous Hyperband and Vizier.
- §4.3 says "nearly trice" (typo for thrice).
- The arXiv abstract-page metadata lists seven authors (omitting Jonathan Ben-Tzur), while the PDF and the MLSys proceedings page list eight.

## Relation to other systems

- **Synchronous SHA / Hyperband:** same rungs, η and brackets; ASHA removes the rung barrier and promotes from partial rungs. Asynchronous Hyperband loops ASHA over several s values; in §4.4 it lags early and catches up at about 1.5 × time(R).
- **BOHB:** hybrid of Bayesian optimization and Hyperband with synchronous rungs; matches ASHA sequentially but is slower in the distributed and NAS experiments (§4.2–4.3).
- **PBT:** parallel; the paper notes no theoretical guarantees, a neural-network focus, and incompatibility with NAS because weights cannot warm-start across architectures (§2, §4.3).
- **Vizier:** the paper says its early stopping offers only about 3× speedup versus order-of-magnitude speedups for SHA (§2).
- **Fabolas:** sequential, multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization; Hyperband (by rung) is competitive with lower variance (Appendix A.2).
- **Ray Tune, Optuna:** the paper notes both already support ASHA (§2); Determined AI is the production host described in §5.

## Takeaways for practice

- Replace rung barriers with a local greedy rule (promote any unpromoted top-1/η config, else start a new one); a few wrong promotions buy immunity to stragglers and drops.
- Treat a tuning job as elastic: its useful parallelism is κ|S| and shrinks as brackets narrow, so reallocate instead of holding static reservations; water-fill fair share with per-job caps so one sweep does not starve others.
- Cap per-trial data parallelism with a scaling-efficiency model; beyond it, GPUs buy more trials.
- Log every promotion and checkpoint full training state so a crashed controller can rebuild rungs.
- Expose random-search-like inputs and hide η and s behind defaults.
