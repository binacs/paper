# Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve

> **Why it matters:** It names the "generation stall" that prefill-first iteration-level batching causes, and shows that splitting prefills into chunks and co-scheduling them with decodes under a fixed per-iteration token budget gives both high throughput and bounded tail time-between-tokens (TBT). Chunked prefill is now a default feature in mainstream LLM serving engines.

## Problem

An LLM request has two phases. **Prefill** processes the whole prompt in one pass and is compute-bound: per-GPU prefill throughput saturates even at batch size one (Fig. 3). **Decode** emits one token per iteration and is memory-bound, so its throughput grows roughly linearly with batch size (Fig. 3). Linear layers dominate runtime: over 80% even at long sequence lengths (Fig. 4). In a decode batch, the linear-layer cost of one token is close to the cost of 128 prefill tokens (Fig. 4). This means decodes can ride along with prefill work almost for free.

The paper sorts existing schedulers into two groups (§2.5, Fig. 2, Fig. 7):

- **Prefill-prioritizing** (Orca, vLLM). Iteration-level batching admits new requests and runs their prefills eagerly. Batches stay large and throughput is high, but every long prefill pauses all ongoing decodes. These pauses are *generation stalls*, and a single one in vLLM can last several seconds (Fig. 1a). P99 TBT rises steeply with load (Fig. 1b).
- **Decode-prioritizing** (FasterTransformer, request-level batching). Running decodes finish before new prefills start, so TBT is low. The batch shrinks as requests complete, which wastes compute and gives low throughput.

A footnote names disaggregation (Splitwise, DistServe) as a third category.

**Pipeline bubbles (§3.3).** Micro-batch runtimes vary widely under pipeline parallelism (PP), and iteration-level scheduling still leaves bubbles. The paper names three kinds:

- **PB1:** consecutive micro-batches carry different numbers of prefill tokens.
- **PB2:** a prefill micro-batch is followed by a decode micro-batch with a much shorter compute time.
- **PB3:** decode attention cost varies with KV-cache length.

For Falcon-180B, a 4k-token prefill (about 1150 ms) interleaved with a decode-only batch of 32 (about 200 ms) can leave a bubble of about 950 ms.

## Core design

### Chunked prefills (§4.1)
- A long prompt is split into near-equal chunks, and each chunk runs in a different iteration. Prefill already saturates the GPU at a few hundred tokens (around 512 on A100, per the Fig. 4 discussion), while real prompts often run to thousands of tokens. Splitting therefore costs little throughput.
- Each later chunk must attend to the KV cache of all earlier chunks. With N chunks, the first chunk's KV is read N−1 times, so smaller chunks mean more repeated KV reads and more kernel-launch overhead.
- **Implementation:** paged chunked-prefill attention kernels from FlashAttention v2 and FlashInfer. The evaluation uses the FlashAttention backend.

### Stall-free batching (§4.2, Algorithm 3)
Each iteration has a **token budget** τ. The scheduler builds a batch in this order:
1. Add every running decode, one token each. Decodes are never preempted to make room for prefill.
2. Add the next chunk of any partially prefilled request, up to the remaining budget.
3. Admit new requests and add their first prefill chunks, up to the remaining budget, if KV memory allows.

The result is a hybrid batch of decodes plus prefill chunks, and the number of tokens per iteration stays roughly constant. Because the budget caps the prefill work any iteration can carry, it also bounds each iteration's latency, and therefore TBT. Hybrid batching without chunking does not solve the problem: putting a full prompt in a decode batch raises TBT by up to 28.3× compared with a decode-only batch (§4.2).

### Token budget selection (§4.3)
- The budget is a static, per-deployment knob chosen from profiling and the P99 TBT SLO. A small τ gives lower TBT but more chunking overhead and a longer TTFT. A large τ gives the reverse.
- The authors pick the budget that maximizes capacity using **Vidur**, their own LLM-inference profiler and simulator.
- **Tile quantization:** a chunk size that does not align with the GEMM tile wastes compute. For example, a 257-token chunk can take 32% longer to prefill than a 256-token chunk, so budgets should be tile multiples.
- In the evaluation, τ is 512 for strict SLOs and 2048 for relaxed SLOs. The one exception is LLaMA2-70B under the relaxed SLO, which uses 1536 (§5.1).

### Pipeline-parallel bubble reduction
Every micro-batch carries about τ tokens, so stage compute times are close to uniform. This mostly removes PB1 and PB2, and a smaller τ also lowers variance between batches. The benefit lets PP across nodes (4-way TP within a node, 2-way PP across nodes) beat 8-way TP that spans nodes over 100 Gbps Ethernet (§5.3).

### Implementation (§4.4)
Sarathi-Serve is built on the vLLM codebase. It adds the scheduling policies, chunked prefills, PP support and telemetry, and uses NCCL for TP and PP communication. The artifact appendix describes it as a research prototype that lacks full feature parity with vLLM.

## Limitations

- **The token budget is static.** One τ per deployment cannot adapt to shifts in workload or SLO, and the paper leaves dynamic budgets to future work (§5.1). Choosing τ depends on offline profiling (Vidur).
- **Chunking costs TTFT.** Chunked prefill is "somewhat slower than full prefills" (§6). Table 4 shows chunked-only gives a higher TTFT than hybrid-only, and long prompts take several iterations to finish prefill. TBT improves at the expense of TTFT.
- **Interference is bounded, not removed.** Prefill and decode still share the same GPUs and KV memory. The paper concedes that disaggregation (Splitwise, DistServe, TetriInfer) can run prefills more efficiently and give a better TTFT. It leaves a quantitative comparison with those systems to future work (§6).
- **Narrow evaluation.** Baselines are only vLLM and Orca (no FastGen, no disaggregated systems); Poisson arrivals, two datasets; no priorities, preemption or multi-tenant fairness.
- **Inconsistent headline numbers.** The abstract's 5.6× for Falcon-180B does not match §5.3 (3.6× strict) or the Fig. 13 caption (4.3×/3.6×); Fig. 12's caption and §5.2's text attribute 3.5× to different models.

## Relation to other systems

- **Orca (OSDI'22):** introduced iteration-level scheduling with selective batching. Sarathi-Serve keeps it but changes what enters each iteration: decodes first, then prefill up to a budget. The paper attributes Orca's lower capacity to stalls, cross-request batching that raises tail latency, and no PagedAttention.
- **vLLM (SOSP'23):** PagedAttention plus a prefill-prioritizing scheduler; Sarathi-Serve's codebase and main baseline. Its strict-SLO capacity is flat across batch sizes because stalls dominate (§5.2).
- **SARATHI (arXiv 2308.16369, same group):** introduced chunked prefills and *decode-maximal batching* (one prefill chunk plus piggybacked decodes), aimed at throughput, with PP bubble reduction for GPT-3. Sarathi-Serve reframes it around tail-TBT SLOs, adding budget-driven stall-free admission, tile-aligned budget selection and an online-serving evaluation; it cites SARATHI only in passing.
- **DeepSpeed-FastGen (arXiv 2401.08671):** per its abstract, uses "Dynamic SplitFuse", a prompt/generation composition strategy in the same family as chunked prefill. The abstract reports gains in throughput and tail latency over vLLM. Sarathi-Serve does not discuss or compare against it.
- **DistServe (OSDI'24) and Splitwise (ISCA'24):** the alternative approach. They place prefill and decode on separate GPU pools and move the KV cache between them, which removes interference entirely and lets each phase be tuned on its own. Sarathi-Serve argues that this needs high-bandwidth interconnect for KV migration and leaves memory on prefill replicas underused. It concedes that disaggregation gives a better TTFT (§6).

## Takeaways for practice

- **Budget tokens per iteration, not requests per batch.** τ is the SLO knob: iteration latency is roughly a function of τ, so P99 TBT becomes predictable. Schedule decodes first; admission control acts only on prefill tokens.
- **Pick τ by profiling (a simulator like Vidur helps) and align it to the GEMM tile**; misaligned chunks waste up to tens of percent of compute (§4.3).
- **Treat TTFT and TBT as separate SLOs.** Chunking moves cost from TBT to TTFT; for long-prompt workloads needing low TTFT, use a larger τ or disaggregation.
- **Uniform iterations make cross-node PP viable** without NVLink-class links; revisit the TP-vs-PP choice when the scheduler changes, since bubbles are partly a scheduling artifact.
- **Measure capacity under a tail-latency SLO** (max QPS at a P99 TBT target with bounded queueing), not raw throughput, which hides stalls.
