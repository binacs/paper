# Federated Optimization in Heterogeneous Networks

> **Why it matters:** FedProx turns "drop the stragglers" into "keep their partial work" and adds a proximal term so that variable, inexact local work still converges, which is a clean model for any synchronous training loop whose workers cannot all finish the same amount of compute per round.

## Problem

Federated learning minimizes a weighted sum of per-device objectives, f(w) = sum over devices k of p_k F_k(w), with p_k = n_k/n (Sec. 3). Two kinds of heterogeneity break the standard method, FedAvg:

- **Systems heterogeneity.** Devices differ in hardware, connectivity and battery (Sec. 2). FedAvg (Algorithm 1) asks each of K sampled devices to run E epochs of SGD, and common practice is to drop devices that fail to finish E epochs within a time window (Sec. 3.1). A larger E raises the share of devices dropped, so computation is wasted and the update is biased toward fast devices.
- **Statistical heterogeneity.** Data are non-identically distributed across devices. With many local epochs, local models drift toward their own optima and FedAvg can diverge (Sec. 1, Sec. 5.3).

Prior FedAvg analyses mostly assume IID data, full participation, or a specific local solver (Sec. 2).

## Core design

### Local objective with a proximal term

Each selected device k approximately minimizes h_k(w; w_t) = F_k(w) plus (mu/2) times the squared distance between w and the current global model w_t (Eq. 2). The penalty pulls local iterates back toward the global model. The paper gives mu two roles (Sec. 3.2):

- it limits client drift under non-IID data without having to hand-tune the number of local epochs (mu acts as a re-parameterization of E);
- it makes it safe to merge updates produced with very different amounts of local work.

With mu chosen large enough, h_k is convex even when F_k is non-convex, and mu-strongly convex when F_k is convex (Sec. 3.2).

### Inexactness as the unit of "partial work"

Definition 2 formalizes how much work a device did: w is a gamma_k^t-inexact solution if the gradient norm of h_k at w is at most gamma_k^t times the gradient norm at the starting point w_t. gamma lies in [0, 1]; smaller means a more accurate local solve. The number of local iterations is the practical proxy (Sec. 3.2). Any local solver works, including a non-iterative one.

### Round structure (Algorithm 2)

1. Server samples K devices, each with probability p_k.
2. Server broadcasts w_t.
3. Each device returns a gamma_k^t-inexact minimizer of h_k, doing as much work as its budget allows.
4. Server averages the returned models with a simple 1/K average.

FedAvg is the special case mu = 0, SGD as local solver and the same gamma for all devices (Sec. 3.2). The paper describes FedProx as "only lightweight modifications to FedAvg" (Sec. 3.2).

### Theory

- **B-local dissimilarity (Def. 3, Assumption 1).** B(w) compares the expected squared norm of local gradients with the squared norm of the global gradient. B(w) = 1 when all local functions are identical; larger B means more dissimilar devices. Assumption 1 bounds B(w) by a constant B away from stationary points.
- **Theorem 4 (non-convex).** If each F_k is L-smooth, its Hessian is bounded below, and mu exceeds that lower-bound constant, then each round decreases the global objective in expectation by rho times the squared gradient norm, with rho a constant depending on mu, gamma, B, L and K. Larger gamma (less local work) and larger B shrink rho; larger K helps. The paper stresses the condition on mu is "sufficient but not necessary" (Sec. 4.2). Remark 5 states sufficient conditions in terms of gamma times B and of B relative to the square root of K (the exact second condition extracted garbled and is not reproduced here).
- **Corollary 9 (variable gamma)** extends Theorem 4 to per-device, per-round gamma_k^t, using the worst gamma among sampled devices that round. This is the formal backing for aggregating straggler partial work.
- **Remark 8 and following text.** In the convex, exact, bounded-variance case the rate matches SGD, and local updating can "perform worse than distributed SGD" with non-IID data. The theory guarantees safety, not a speedup.

### Adaptive mu heuristic

Mu is increased by 0.1 when the loss increases and decreased by 0.1 when the loss keeps decreasing for 5 rounds (Sec. 5.3.2, Fig. 3, App. C.3.3).

## Limitations

- **Simulated, round-counted systems heterogeneity.** Stragglers are emulated by assigning random epoch counts on one machine; results are reported "in terms of rounds rather than FLOPs or wall-clock time" (Sec. 5.1). There is no measurement of real device latency, network cost, or round time.
- **Synchronous rounds only.** A global clock bounds each round (Sec. 5.2). Devices that do zero work, or never respond, are outside the gamma framework (gamma = 1 means no progress), and asynchrony is not studied.
- **No speedup guarantee.** The bound matches SGD at best and the paper notes local updating can be worse than distributed SGD with non-IID data (Sec. 4.2). Conditions are sufficient, not necessary.
- **mu needs tuning.** Best mu ranges from 0.001 to 1 across datasets; automatic tuning is left as future work (Sec. 5.3.2). The adaptive rule is a heuristic without analysis.
- **Small scale.** Logistic regression and small LSTMs, 10 devices per round; privacy is out of scope (footnote 1).
- **Inconsistencies inside the paper.**
  - Algorithms 1–2 sample devices proportionally to p_k and take a simple average, but experiments default to uniform sampling with sample-weighted averaging (Sec. 5.1, App. C.2), even though Sec. 5.1 says the proposed scheme gives more stable performance and Fig. 12 shows it slightly better.
  - FEMNIST is described as a 62-class EMNIST variant in Sec. 5.1, while App. C.1 describes subsampling 10 lowercase characters with 5 classes per device.
  - Sent140 uses word-level GloVe embeddings, yet App. C.1 says the model "takes as input a sequence of 25 characters."
  - The adaptive-mu rule is worded slightly differently in Sec. 5.3.2 than in Fig. 3 and App. C.3.3 (the 5-round condition for decreasing appears only in the latter).

## Relation to other systems

- **FedAvg.** FedProx with mu = 0 and uniform gamma is FedAvg. The key systems difference is aggregation of partial work instead of dropping stragglers; Sec. 5.2 reports that FedProx with mu = 0 alone already gives "more stable and faster convergence" than dropping (Sec. 5.2, Fig. 1).
- **DANE / AIDE.** Use a similar proximal term plus a gradient-correction term but assume all devices participate every round (Sec. 2). The authors' federated variant, FedDane, "performs uniformly worse" than FedProx on heterogeneous data and "tends to diverge" (App. B, Fig. 4).
- **EASGD.** Similar proximal coupling, but analyzed only for simple quadratics (Sec. 2).
- **Distributed / mini-batch SGD.** The theoretical baseline; FedProx matches but does not beat its rate (Sec. 4.2).
- **SCAFFOLD and later drift-correction methods.** (Own analysis) Later work corrects client drift with control variates rather than a proximal penalty; FedProx is the usual baseline such work compares against. This is not discussed in the paper.
- **Backup workers / bounded-staleness data-parallel training.** (Own analysis) Datacenter systems handle stragglers by dropping slow workers' gradients or tolerating staleness. FedProx is a third option: bound the wall-clock round, accept whatever each worker computed, and make the objective robust to the mix.

## Takeaways for practice

- (Own analysis) Treat "work done per round" as a variable, not a constant. Fix the round deadline and let each worker contribute what it finished; this converts tail latency into lower per-worker progress instead of lost work.
- (Own analysis) If workers do unequal numbers of local steps, add a regularizer that bounds how far a local replica can move from the shared model. The paper's evidence is that partial work alone helps, and the proximal term is what restores convergence under high heterogeneity (Fig. 1 caption, Sec. 5.2).
- (Own analysis) Track a dissimilarity signal. The paper uses the variance of local gradients (Sec. 5.3.3, Fig. 2, Fig. 8) and shows it tracks convergence; a scheduler or controller could use the same signal to set mu or the local-step budget.
- (Own analysis) Do not expect a convergence speedup from local steps under non-IID data; the gain is robustness and use of straggler compute, not faster rate (Sec. 4.2).
- (Own analysis) Results are in rounds on a simulator; validate wall-clock benefits on real fleets before using them for capacity planning.
