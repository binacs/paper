# Splitwise: Efficient Generative LLM Inference Using Phase Splitting

> **Why it matters:** One of the two founding prefill/decode disaggregation papers. It argues from production traces and power and cost data that the two phases belong on different machine pools, possibly on different GPU generations, and sizes whole clusters under iso-power and iso-cost budgets.

## Problem

The prompt (prefill) phase processes all input tokens in parallel. It is compute-bound, and its latency (TTFT) grows almost linearly with prompt length. The token (decode) phase emits one token per step, is bound by memory bandwidth and capacity, and dominates end-to-end time. vLLM-style mixed continuous batching runs both phases on the same H100 machines. As a result, prefill interferes with TBT, decode wastes expensive compute, and power and cost are provisioned for the most demanding phase. The paper asks whether splitting the phases across machines, and choosing different hardware for each, gives better throughput per dollar and per watt while still meeting SLOs on TTFT, TBT and E2E.

## Core design

**Characterization (Sec. III).** The study uses 20-minute production traces from two Azure LLM services, *coding* and *conversation*, and runs BLOOM-176B and Llama2-70B on vLLM on 8×H100 machines. It reaches seven insights:
- Coding has long prompts and very short outputs. Conversation has longer, nearly bimodal outputs.
- Under mixed batching, decode batches are usually small, so decode badly underuses compute.
- Prefill throughput saturates at about 2048 batched tokens. Decode throughput scales with batch size until KV-cache memory runs out.
- Prefill power rises with batch size and is sensitive to caps. Decode power is flat, and decode latency barely changes under a deep cap.
- A100 decodes at a modest TBT penalty versus H100, with better perf/$ and perf/W.

**Three pools.** A *prompt pool* runs only prefill and a *token pool* only decode. A *mixed pool* expands and shrinks with load and runs ordinary mixed continuous batching. Machines keep a home role and return home once no opposite-type work remains in their queue.

**Cluster-level scheduler (CLS).** Join-the-Shortest-Queue on arrival, with queue length counted in *pending tokens*. The CLS picks the prompt and token machine together at admission, so the KV transfer can overlap prefill. Above a queue threshold it uses the mixed pool. If that is also loaded, it pulls a machine from the opposite pool into the mixed pool. Repurposing is coarse-grained and infrequent.

**Machine-level scheduler (MLS).**
- *Prompt machines:* FCFS, batching up to 2048 total tokens, the throughput knee.
- *Token machines:* FCFS, batching as much as memory allows, then queueing.
- *Mixed machines:* prompts take priority and can preempt decode. Priority rises with age, and preemptions per request are capped to prevent starvation.

**KV-cache transfer (Sec. IV-C, V-A).** Instead of a serialized transfer after prefill, Splitwise starts an asynchronous transfer of each layer's KV cache as soon as that layer finishes, overlapping it with the remaining prefill. Small prompts (under 512 tokens on H100) fall back to serialized transfer, where synchronization overhead would dominate. The vLLM prototype uses MSCCL++ zero-copy one-sided puts over InfiniBand with per-request semaphores. The transfer is lossless.

**Provisioning (Sec. IV-D, V-B).** SplitwiseSim, an event-driven simulator with a profiled performance model, searches prompt and token machine counts. It maximizes throughput or minimizes cost or power, subject to all nine SLOs (P50, P90 and P99 of TTFT, TBT and E2E), defined as slowdowns versus an uncontended DGX-A100. The designs are Splitwise-AA (A100 for both pools), HH (H100 for both), HHcap (HH with token machines capped at 70% power) and HA (H100 prompt, A100 token). The baselines are homogeneous A100 and H100 clusters with mixed continuous batching.

## Limitations

- **Interconnect assumption.** Splitwise-HA needs InfiniBand between H100 and A100 machines, which is not readily available. The authors only argue that a link with 10× less bandwidth would likely still help. Mixing GPU generations in one datacenter also adds fragmentation and operational cost.
- **Narrow evidence base.** One 20-minute trace window, two services, two dense models, two NVIDIA GPUs. Cluster-scale results are simulated, and the vLLM prototype was not fully artifact-validated.
- **No prefix or KV reuse.** KV-cache reuse is disabled. The authors note that multi-turn chat with cached context could change the memory behavior of the prompt phase. Later systems such as Mooncake showed this changes the economics of the design.
- **Coarse elasticity.** Pools re-balance only via the mixed pool, and there is no per-phase choice of tensor or pipeline parallelism.
- **Fault tolerance and scale.** When a machine fails, requests restart from scratch, and KV checkpointing is left out of scope. The centralized CLS may become a bottleneck at scale.

## Relation to other systems

- **DistServe (OSDI'24).** Both papers disaggregate prefill and decode, and both take SLOs on TTFT and TPOT/TBT as first-class constraints. They differ in which axis they optimize.
  - *Splitwise* optimizes the *hardware and cluster* axis. It provisions whole machines (DGX-A100 or DGX-H100) per pool, mixes GPU generations, power-caps decode, and reports results under iso-power and iso-cost budgets. Placement is a machine-count search. Parallelism stays fixed per machine.
  - *DistServe* optimizes the *model-parallelism and placement* axis on homogeneous GPUs. It searches a separate tensor- and pipeline-parallel configuration and replica count for each phase to maximize per-GPU goodput. On low-bandwidth clusters it co-locates matching prefill and decode stages within a node, so the KV cache moves over NVLink.
  - *Interconnect.* Splitwise assumes fast cross-machine InfiniBand and hides the transfer with layer-wise streaming. DistServe makes placement depend on the bandwidth it has.
  - *Elasticity.* Splitwise's mixed pool falls back to colocated mixed batching under load spikes. DistServe has no mixed pool and relies on re-planning.
  
  The two are complementary. DistServe-style per-phase parallelism could run inside Splitwise-style heterogeneous pools.
- **vLLM / Orca.** The baselines. Splitwise's mixed pool is exactly their mixed continuous batching, the colocation the paper blames for wasted decode compute and power.
- **Sarathi-Serve (OSDI'24).** It attacks the same prefill/decode interference without disaggregating, using chunked prefill and stall-free batching on one machine. It avoids KV transfer and heterogeneous fleets, but it cannot give decode cheaper or power-capped hardware.
- **TetriInfer / DéjàVu (2024).** Contemporaneous disaggregation designs focused on scheduling (length prediction, chunked prefill) and KV streaming for pipelined serving. Splitwise is the hardware and power provisioning view of the same idea.
- **Mooncake (FAST'25).** It is a KV-cache-centric disaggregated architecture with a distributed KV store and prefix reuse. That is exactly the cached-context case Splitwise leaves for future work.

## Takeaways for practice

- Characterize per phase before you provision. Prefill and decode differ in compute, memory and power profiles. One SKU and one batching policy for both leaves throughput per dollar and per watt on the table.
- Decode is a natural fit for older GPUs or power-capped GPUs. If decode latency hardly changes under deep power caps, decode pools can raise rack density within a fixed power envelope.
- Measure scheduler queue length in pending tokens, not requests, and pick the prefill and decode machines together, at admission time, so the KV transfer can be overlapped.
- Stream the KV cache layer by layer, overlapped with prefill, for long prompts. Fall back to serialized transfer for short prompts, where synchronization overhead dominates. Watch the second-token latency, which the transfer hits hardest.
- Keep a colocated fallback, such as the mixed pool, to absorb shifts in load mix. Hardware chosen for one workload (HA, HHcap) is the least robust when the mix changes.
- Size clusters with a validated simulator against all latency percentiles, and state which constraint (iso-power, iso-cost or iso-throughput) is fixed. Headline numbers depend on it. A mixed-generation fleet also needs a fast link between generations.
